## One-line description (about 20 words)

> Torchbearer builds the measurement layer social programmes are missing, so organisations can prove what
> changed, not just what they spent.

---

## The problem (200 to 300 words)

> Saudi Arabia's non-profit sector grew from around SAR 8 billion before 2016 to roughly SAR 73 billion in
> 2024, and passed its 2030 target of one million volunteers six years early. Those numbers are real and they
> are impressive. They are also all inputs. Riyals committed, organisations registered, volunteers signed up.
> None of them tells you what changed for anybody.
>
> The reason is not laziness. Environmental reporting has a common unit, so a tonne of CO2 in Riyadh means the
> same thing as a tonne in Jeddah. Social reporting has no equivalent. So it falls back on proxies: training
> hours delivered, sessions run, people reached. Those describe activity, not outcome.
>
> The part that makes this urgent is that it cannot be fixed afterwards. If a programme ran for three years
> without measuring where people started, no analyst can create that baseline later. The data simply does not
> exist. Measurement has to be designed before delivery, or the result is not defensible.
>
> Meanwhile the requirement is tightening. Saudi disclosure rules became binding for green and
> sustainability-linked debt issuers in 2025, after voluntary guidelines in 2019 and a Tadawul framework in
> 2021, and ISSB-aligned standards are in development through SOCPA. In 2024, 94 listed companies published
> sustainability reports, most of them voluntarily. The regulator for the non-profit sector has publicly moved
> its own strategy from growing the sector to professionalising it.
>
> So a growing number of organisations are about to be asked to evidence social outcomes to a standard that
> their current reporting does not meet, and most of their delivery partners cannot help them.

## Your solution (200 to 300 words)

> Torchbearer Verify is a platform that carries a social programme from design through to evidence an auditor
> can accept.
>
> Three parts. An indicator builder, where a programme is defined against named SDG targets and Vision 2030
> pillars, and the baseline instruments are generated before delivery starts. A field capture layer that works
> offline, in the participant's own language, and records attrition and exclusions as they happen rather than
> reconstructing them at the end. And an evidence pack that assembles raw instruments, method notes and every
> adjustment, so a third party can rebuild the result without talking to us.
>
> What makes this different from ESG reporting software is that we start before the programme does. Reporting
> tools aggregate whatever they are given. They cannot fix a missing baseline. We are selling the discipline
> that makes the number exist in the first place.
>
> The method behind it is already written and published on our site: five stages, with a standard attached to
> each, and eleven analytical papers where every figure is cited and linked to source. We publish what
> underperformed as well as what worked. What we have not done is turn it into software, which is the specific
> thing Launchpad is for.
>
> We are not asking anyone to trust a framework we invented in a room. We run programmes in flood-exposed
> districts of Pakistan, where supply chains are long, service coverage is thin and the households that most
> need an intervention are on nobody's list. A measurement approach that survives there transfers downwards in
> difficulty rather than upwards.

## Target customer (150 words)

> Four buyers, in the order we expect to reach them.
>
> Corporates facing sustainability disclosure with a social pillar they cannot currently evidence. They have
> the mandate and the budget and no delivery capability, which is the gap we sit in.
>
> Non-profits inside the National Center for Non-Profit Sector's professionalisation agenda, who now have to
> demonstrate measurable impact rather than activity.
>
> Funds and development finance institutions that need results in a defined framework rather than a narrative
> report.
>
> Government and public sector entities building measurement capability they intend to keep in-house.
>
> Our beachhead is the first group. A corporate with a disclosure obligation has a dated problem, a budget line
> and a named person accountable for solving it. That combination makes for a much shorter sales conversation
> than selling better practice to an organisation with no external pressure.

## Business model (150 words)

> Three revenue lines that build on each other.
>
> We land with a design engagement. Fixed scope, fixed price: programme design, measurement plan, indicator
> mapping and a cost-per-outcome model. It stands on its own, and the client is free to go and deliver it with
> anyone. It is deliberately small, because it produces the document that makes the platform obviously
> necessary.
>
> We expand with a per-programme licence for Verify. That is the recurring line and the reason this is a
> venture rather than a consultancy.
>
> We deepen with delivery and verification: full execution through local partners, or independent verification
> of programmes already running.
>
> Nobody buys measurement software until they have seen what their current numbers cannot survive. So the
> sequence matters more than the pricing. We show them the gap first.

---

## Competition and differentiation (150 words)

> Global consultancies design and sometimes verify, but they do not deliver, so their frameworks are written by
> people who have never had to execute one. Implementing NGOs deliver, but design measurement after the fact,
> which is when it can no longer be repaired. ESG reporting software aggregates whatever it is handed and
> cannot fix a missing baseline. Assurance firms arrive at the end, when the evidence either exists or does
> not.
>
> We do all three, and we built outwards from field delivery, which is the only order that works. You can add
> measurement to a delivery organisation. You cannot add delivery credibility to a software company.
>
> The defensible part is not the code. It is that the code encodes a method proven in genuinely difficult
> conditions, and that we keep delivering, so it stays honest.
